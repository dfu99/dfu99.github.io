title: "Where αVβ3 Flexes"

Integrin αVβ3 is a cell-surface receptor built from two chains, αV and β3, running alongside each other. Each has a globular head at the top and a long leg below, folded at a knee about halfway down, and the molecule works by straightening from a compact folded form into an upright one. A useful question about a machine like this is which parts of it actually move, and which are simply carried along.

Two independent sources answer it here. One is a library of 615 structures generated by simulation, described in a [companion post](/2026/09/02/a-conformer-library-steered-md.html). The other is 1645 frames of a real molecule, recovered by fitting structures to two high-speed AFM recordings, described in [another](/2026/09/02/c-hs-afm-template-matching.html). This report covers the flexibility map built from both, its agreement with a published calculation from 2008, how the map changes depending on what shape the molecule is in, and a negative result on whether straightening opens up any new drug-binding pocket.

## Three measures of flexibility, and an artefact that had to be removed

Flexibility was measured three ways, once per amino acid, so that a residue only counts as genuinely mobile if all three agree.

The first is how far each residue moves over time in the real recordings. The second is how much each residue's position varies across the simulated library, after superimposing every pair of structures as closely as possible. The third measures bending rather than movement: the angle formed at each residue by its neighbours along the backbone, and how much that angle varies. The third matters because it is immune to a problem that afflicts the first two. If the whole molecule swings, every residue in the swinging part registers as mobile whether or not anything bent, whereas an angle at a residue is unchanged by moving the molecule as a whole.

That problem is not hypothetical: raw movement in the recordings is dominated by the molecule turning on the surface. Holding the head fixed by superimposing every frame on the head before measuring, so that only motion relative to the head counts, drops the average from 53.5 to 19.3 Å in recording 1 and from 71.2 to 19.3 Å in recording 2. Before the correction the two recordings disagreed by 18 Å; after it they agree to three significant figures. The disagreement was entirely an artefact of the longer recording having more time to rotate.

With rotation removed, the head moves by 7.4 to 9.7 Å, which is about what surface loops and side chains do on a body that is otherwise rigid. The mobility rises down the legs: about 20 Å in the αV calf, 26 Å in the β3 lower leg, and 44 to 47 Å in the disordered tail at the very end of the αV chain, nearest where the membrane would be.

![Three-metric mechanical sensitivity composite](/images/2026-09-02/mechanical_sensitivity_composite_v2.png)
*Movement in the real recordings with rotation removed (top), positional variability across the simulated library (second), backbone angle variability (third), and the combination of all three (bottom), with the ten residues scoring highest on all three circled and the twelve switch residues proposed by Matsumoto and colleagues in 2008 marked by how well they match.*

The bottom panel multiplies the three scores together, so that a residue ranks highly only by scoring on all of them; a residue that is mobile by one measure and static by the others scores near zero. The highest values fall at β3 residue 689, β3 652 and αV 842, all in the disordered tails at the ends of the chains. Those tails dominate the ranking. The classical way of predicting which parts of a protein move works from a single static structure and does not pick this up, because the motion involves the flexible ends flopping against the surface rather than the folded body flexing.

Whether that ranking would survive a different sample was tested by repeatedly recomputing it from randomly redrawn subsets of the frames, 500 times. The two recordings produce nearly identical flexibility profiles, correlating at 0.998. The 19 most flexible residues keep their place in at least 97 % of the redraws. The 20th and 21st cannot be separated, because their uncertainty ranges overlap, so the top 19 is the ranking worth quoting and the next handful is a second tier. All of them lie in the lower αV leg and the region nearest the membrane, at residues 761 to 764, 804 to 805, 909 to 915 and 956 to 962.

## Agreement with a published prediction from 2008

In 2008 Matsumoto and colleagues proposed twelve residues in αVβ3 as the switches controlling its shape change, using a method that treats the protein as a network of springs and solves for its natural vibration modes. That calculation and the measurements here share no data and no assumptions, so agreement between them is a real check. Each of the twelve was looked up in the backbone-angle variability map and ranked against all other residues.

![Matsumoto 2008 switch residues on the angular variance map](/images/2026-09-02/matsumoto_overlay.png)
*Backbone angle variability per residue for the αV chain (top) and the β3 chain (bottom), with the twelve residues proposed by Matsumoto and colleagues marked and coloured by how well they match.*

Two land in the top 5 % of all residues, three in the top 20 %, one has a match within ten residues, and six do not match at all. A 50 % hit rate would be unremarkable on its own. What makes it informative is which six miss.

The twelve do not all play the same role in the 2008 model. Five are proposed to act as backbone hinges, and all five land at or above the 80th percentile here, including the residue identified as the main trigger, β3 Arg633, and the pair Cys374 and Leu375 at the 99.4th and 97.2nd. The six that miss are all residues proposed to hold parts of the structure together by contact rather than to bend, so a measure of bending should not flag them, and it does not. The two methods measure different things, and they agree wherever they measure the same thing.

Separately, the single most variable backbone angle in the entire library, at 25.4°, falls at β3 residue 353, in the knee of the β3 leg. The prediction written down before the calculation was run named residue 352.

## Flexibility depends on which shape the molecule is in

The map above averages over the whole recording, during which the molecule was sometimes folded and sometimes upright. Labelling each frame by which shape it was in, using the state assignment from a [companion post](/2026/09/02/d-hs-afm-kinetics-and-free-energy.html), allows the map to be recomputed separately for each.

![Per-residue mobility split by conformational state](/images/2026-09-02/rmsf_per_state_v1.png)
*Per-residue mobility measured separately in the bent, intermediate and upright states, with uncertainty bands (top), the difference between upright and bent per residue (bottom left), the two plotted against each other (bottom right), and the ten residues whose mobility depends most on state.*

Average mobility is 19.7 Å when folded, 14.9 Å in the intermediate and 12.4 Å when upright, so the folded molecule is 60 % more mobile than the upright one. The difference is not marginal: 1640 of 1654 residues change significantly.

What does not change is which residues are the mobile ones. Compare any two states and the per-residue profiles correlate above 0.99, so the lower-leg hotspot is the most mobile region whether the molecule is folded or upright, and only the amount changes. The residues that change most are the same ones, losing up to 24.9 Å of mobility at αV residue 764 as the molecule straightens. A plausible reading is that the folded molecule can rock between several slightly different folded arrangements, while the upright one is committed to a single geometry.

The same asymmetry shows up in the overall shape. Three standard measures were used: overall size, how far the shape departs from a sphere, and how close it is to a pure rod. All three move the same way when the molecule straightens. Size rises from 53 to 70 Å, departure from spherical roughly doubles, and the rod measure rises from 0.59 to 0.78 on a scale where 1 is a perfect rod. Eleven of the twelve comparisons between states are statistically significant after correcting for the number of tests.

More informative than the averages is the spread. Every one of the three measures varies two to three times more widely in the folded state than in the upright one. The folded molecule is not one shape but a family of them; the upright molecule is one shape. This matches the state model preferring to split the bent state in two and not the others.

## Which parts move apart when the molecule straightens

If the molecule straightens, something must move apart from something else. Which parts was answered by measuring the distance between the centres of ten pairs of domains in each state, and asking which pairs separate.

![Per-state domain-pair distances](/images/2026-09-02/domain_pair_distances_per_state_v1.png)
*(A) The distance between the centres of ten pairs of domains, measured separately in each state. (B) The same pairs ranked by how much they separate between the folded and upright states. (C, D) The full distributions for the pair that separates most and the pair that separates least, with the complete table below.*

Two pairs separate by more than 40 Å, and both are head-from-foot measurements: the αV foot pulling away from the β3 head, and the αV head pulling away from its own foot. The pair that barely moves is the two heads, which separate by 3.3 Å, from 35.0 to 38.3 Å. The two feet stay within 9.5 Å of each other and the two legs within 5.6 Å.

So the molecule straightens by separating its head from its legs, not by separating the heads from each other or the legs from each other. The head travels as one piece and the legs travel together. That is the defining claim of the accepted structural model of integrin activation, and it is recovered here from the fitted recordings without having been assumed anywhere in the fitting.

The same question at finer grain: which residues stop touching. Counting every pair of residues within 8 Å of each other across the head region gives 9083 such contacts when folded, 7750 in the intermediate and 6697 when upright, a 26 % loss. Requiring a contact to change from mostly present to mostly absent, 93 break and 29 form.

The broken ones are not scattered. They cluster in the β3 ligand-binding loop, the helix that shifts when that site is activated, and the propeller-shaped αV head domain. One of them is the direct contact between the αV knee and the β3 head, which is precisely the contact that holds the molecule folded over. The contacts that form are all within the αV upper leg, which is what opening a hinge does to the domain on either side of it.

Counting only the contacts that are present most of the time changes the picture. Those fall by just 2.8 %, against 26 % when every fleeting contact is counted, so the molecule mostly loosens its weak contacts rather than breaking its firm ones. A measure of how tightly each residue's neighbours are interconnected is unchanged. Individual residues do trade partners, with 275 ending up with fewer neighbours and 104 with more, the losses concentrated in the αV head and the gains in the β3 domains nearest the membrane.

## A search for a drug pocket that opens only in one state

A shape change that buries and exposes surfaces can create a pocket that exists in only one state, which is attractive as a drug target because a molecule binding it would act only on the activated receptor. Whether αVβ3 has one was tested three independent ways on 20 folded and 20 upright structures.

The first measures how much of each residue's surface is exposed to solvent, and finds 217 residues becoming more exposed and 103 less so. Most of those are trivial: any two parts that move apart become more exposed, which says nothing about pockets. Keeping only changes within a single domain leaves 9 candidates, and requiring a candidate to look like a drug site, meaning at least five residues and a substantially greasy surface, leaves exactly one: a five-residue patch on β3 at the hinge below the head, gaining 237 Å² of exposure.

The test is sensitive enough for the negative to mean something. It resolves changes an order of magnitude smaller than a real pocket would produce, so a genuine site would not have been missed.

Two further tests rule it out. Exposure alone cannot distinguish a pocket from a flat patch, so the second measures enclosed empty volume directly. The candidate region loses 3153 Å³ of it on straightening, more than a flat control patch does, and in the opposite direction to the receptor's known binding site, which gains. The third scores how well a drug-like molecule could be accommodated, and finds no significant change for the candidate while both controls change significantly.

All three agree on what happens, and it is not pocket formation. The region does change, going from buried with small internal voids to openly exposed. An enclosed cavity did not appear; a surface was uncovered. Those are different things, and only the first is a drug target.

The receptor's real binding site moves the other way, which was not expected. It becomes 35 % less exposed as the molecule straightens, dropping from 459 to 298 Å², and scores worse for accommodating a ligand, even though the legs retreat 52 Å from it and nothing is physically in the way. The cause is local: as the molecule straightens, the two head domains rotate against each other and partly cover the site.

This is a consequence of what the library contains. Its upright structures all have the heads still packed together, and the further step that separates them is the one that actually exposes the binding site. The library never takes that step, so within it, straightening buries the site rather than revealing it.

## What remains

Every result above comes from a library that contains no structure with an open headpiece, and from fits made against that library, so all of it describes the folded-to-upright transition and none of it describes the final activating step. Any question about ligand access in the fully activated receptor is blocked until those structures exist.

The backbone-angle map comes from the simulated library, which was produced by pushing the molecule rather than watching it, so strictly it reports which angles the pushing was able to move. Those need not be the same as the angles that move on their own. Comparing against a simulation with nothing pushing would separate the two.
