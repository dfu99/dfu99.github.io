High-speed atomic force microscopy records a movie of a single molecule as a sequence of low-resolution height maps. It shows that the molecule is changing shape but not what shape it is in, because the images are blurred by the width of the scanning tip and carry about a nanometre of vertical noise.

This half of the method recovers the structure. For each frame it searches a library of candidate structures of αVβ3 integrin, built by simulation and described in a [companion post](/2026/09/02/a-conformer-library-steered-md.html), and returns the structure and the orientation whose predicted image best matches the observed one. The result is a frame-by-frame structural trajectory of one molecule, obtained without training a network and without any tuning per dataset beyond the tip radius. This report covers the two recordings, the four errors that had to be removed before the fit was trustworthy, and the experiment showing that the fit reflects the data rather than the library.

## The recordings, and why a trained network did not work

Two recordings of surface-bound αVβ3 from the Linz high-speed AFM group were used, of 409 and 1296 frames at one frame per second, so about 6 and 21 minutes of continuous observation of one molecule. The first 30 frames of each are discarded because the scanner is still settling, leaving 379 and 1266 frames, called recording 1 and recording 2 below.

The first approach was to train a neural network to read a frame and output the molecule's dimensions directly, using simulated images as training data because the real recordings carry no structural labels. On real data the network outputs nearly the same numbers whatever it is shown. This is not a training failure, since the same network separates the simulated images it learned from. It is a gap between simulated and real images: the network keyed on features of the simulation that the real instrument does not reproduce.

The method used instead needs no training. Each real frame is compared directly against a rendered image of every structure in the library, and the best match wins. That reaches per-frame agreement up to 0.967 on the same data.

## Fitting a structure to each frame

A structure can be laid down on the surface in any orientation, and the image depends on which way it faces as much as on its shape. So each frame is first matched by image correlation against a rendering of every structure in the library, and the winning structure is then re-rendered in 2048 orientations spread evenly over all possible 3D rotations to find the best one. On a GPU this takes about 10 minutes per recording, and about 4 hours on a CPU.

Run in that form the fit gives mean agreement of 0.691 on recording 1 and 0.797 on recording 2, and in 37.8 % of frames the fitted structure is placed head-for-tail relative to its neighbours, which a real molecule cannot do between one second and the next. Four separate corrections turn this into a usable trajectory.

**Anchor on the head, not the centre.** The fitted structure has to be positioned on the image before it can be scored, and it was being placed by its centre of mass. That is the wrong landmark: the legs are long and mobile, so as they swing they drag the centre of mass around and offset the whole structure by more than 2 nm. Locating the molecule's head in each image instead, by smoothing the frame and taking the centre of its brightest connected region, and then anchoring the structure's head there, raises mean agreement to 0.969 and 0.941. The head is the right landmark because it is the stable one, drifting only 0.13 nm per frame. Tip radius was also swept at 1, 2, 3 and 5 nm, which changes agreement only between 0.93 and 0.98; 2 nm is used.

**Render the whole library, not a tenth of it.** The rendered images the matcher searches were generated by a routine whose batching parameters caused it to stop after the first 31 of 309 structures. The images being searched therefore covered head-to-calf distances from 78.6 to 80.8 Å, against the library's true range of 52.9 to 85.0 Å. Ninety per cent of the library was never offered to the matcher, which could only return the extended structures it had been shown. Correcting the batching covers the full library. Nothing about this failed visibly: the fit ran, returned matches, and produced plausible trajectories, and every result predating the fix was invalid.

**Fix head-for-tail frames against the image, not against the previous frame.** The routine that detects and corrects head-for-tail placements compared each frame to the one before it. Comparing to a moving reference lets small errors compound: the structure crept away from the molecule by 1.01 nm on average and up to 10.44 nm, with 399 of the 1266 frames of recording 2 displaced by more than a nanometre. Judging each frame against the head position measured in the image itself removes the drift completely, and shows that no corrections were needed in the first place. The routine had been damaging coordinates that were already right.

Once the head is anchored, one ambiguity is left: the structure can be turned 180° about the vertical axis through the head, which puts the legs on the wrong side. Comparing each frame's leg direction to the running median of its 21 neighbours catches these, correcting 108 frames in recording 1 (28.5 %) and 229 in recording 2 (18.1 %).

**Smooth the coordinates, not the choice of structure.** Fitting every frame independently makes the structure twitch, because the orientation search will happily rotate a little further to chase image noise. The first attempt to fix this replaced each frame's structure with whichever structure was most common in a window around it, while keeping that frame's own orientation. This made the twitching worse, by 11 % and 19 %, because a different structure held at the previous one's orientation is itself a jump.

Smoothing the fitted atom positions directly instead, by taking a running median over 7 frames, cuts the frame-to-frame movement from 45.2 to 10.5 Å and from 38.8 to 9.9 Å. Seven frames is 0.7 s, well short of the shape changes being measured, which take seconds. The median does pull the head position sideways by about 1 Å per frame, invisible over the 379 frames of recording 1 but accumulating over the 1266 of recording 2, so the head is re-anchored after smoothing.

The final fits are 379 frames at mean correlation 0.965 for recording 1, and 1266 frames at mean correlation 0.939 for recording 2.

## The fit responds to the data, not to the library

A method that picks the best match from a fixed set can only ever return something from that set, so it will produce a confident-looking answer even if the right structure was never in the library. The question is whether the answer carries information about the recording at all. The test is to change the library and see whether the two recordings respond to the change differently. If the library were dictating the answer, both would move the same way.

The extension-only library contains 309 structures from the extend-steering run and no bent structures. The complete library adds 306 bent-steering structures, giving 615. Both recordings were re-fitted against each. Frames are banded by the αV head-to-calf centroid distance, with bent-closed below 50 Å, intermediate from 50 to 70 Å, and extended-closed above 70 Å.

| complete library (615 structures) | 43.5 % | 18.6 % |

The two recordings start from the same bent fraction under the incomplete library and diverge under the complete one: recording 1 shifts by 31 percentage points, recording 2 by 6. Adding the same structures to the library moved one recording five times as far as the other, which it could only do by matching frames that were already there in one recording and not the other. This is the evidence that the fit reads the recordings.

The same result carries a warning. The bent fractions measured against the incomplete library, 12.4 % and 12.5 %, were not measurements of the molecule; they were measurements of what the library could offer, and they looked entirely reasonable at the time. Any state fraction from this method is only as complete as the library behind it. The same limit applies with full force to the extended-open form, for which the library contains no structures at all, so its absence from these recordings cannot be distinguished from an inability to represent it.

## What remains

One control committed to in advance has not been run: assigning a structure to each frame at random and confirming that the resulting agreement collapses, to below 0.4. If a random assignment scored nearly as well as the fit, the agreement numbers would be measuring the general resemblance of any blob to any other blob rather than a real match. It costs a few CPU hours and should be done before publication.

The whole result rests on two recordings from one instrument in one laboratory. A recording from a second source would test directly whether the tip and noise settings transfer.

Running the same recordings against structures from AlphaFold alone, at roughly 50 GPU-hours, would establish how much of the result requires the simulated library and how much a structure predictor could have supplied.

Finally, a caution about the most persuasive-looking output. Drawing the fitted structure on top of the real frame produces a video in which the atoms sit neatly on the imaged molecule, and that video proves nothing, because the drawing is made from the same coordinates that were fitted to that frame. It has to line up. The only real measures of fit quality are the agreements against the real images, 0.965 and 0.939.
